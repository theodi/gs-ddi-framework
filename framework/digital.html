Digital

Working effectively in a digital workplace - digital tools, accessible digital content, user-centred digital services, leading in a digital age and handling information well. This domain is here because most civil service work is delivered through digital channels and systems. Nearly everyone needs foundational digital confidence, be it using email applications or collaboration tools. Service design, transformation and leadership apply when you shape how teams or services work.

Questions are grouped as knowledge, skills or behaviours and rated against five level descriptors. Both are described on the proficiency levels page. Some questions are also tagged with broader skills.

Clusters

  1. Foundational Digital Skills

Foundational Digital Skills

Using digital tools effectively

Uses the software, platforms and online tools needed for modern workplace tasks - including communicating, collaborating, managing information and working securely - with confidence and good judgement in any work setting.

Skills

How would you rate your competence in using digital tools and software to complete everyday tasks?

Broader skills: Continuous Learning

1. Novice
Follows instructions to use simple digital tools for everyday tasks such as logging in, finding basic information or joining an online meeting. Needs help when problems occur but shows curiosity and asks questions to learn.
2. Awareness
Uses common digital tools with support to complete basic tasks such as editing a shared document or updating an online task list. Recognises how digital tools help teams stay connected and asks for help when unsure.
3. Working
Applies digital tools independently to complete routine tasks such as joining online meetings, sharing documents and tracking progress. Applies agreed ways of working and fixes simple issues to keep work running smoothly.
4. Practitioner
Organises and independently manages work across multiple digital tools and platforms. Sets up shared spaces, keeps information easy to find and models good online practice. Solves most everyday digital issues and supports colleagues.
5. Expert
Leads on identifying how digital tools and platforms should be best used within the team. Designs simple, consistent ways of working online, promotes secure and inclusive use, and drives improvements that make work quicker and clearer.
How would you rate your competence in managing and organising information and files so they are easy to find, appropriately shared and kept secure?

Broader skills: Collaborative Working

1. Novice
Follows instructions to save and find files in shared spaces but needs support to organise information or apply security settings.
2. Awareness
Uses basic file management steps with guidance - saves documents in the right place, follows naming conventions and checks sharing permissions before sending.
3. Working
Keeps information well-organised and easy to find across shared spaces. Applies agreed security and sharing practices consistently and helps colleagues do the same.
4. Practitioner
Reviews and improves how information is organised and shared across a team. Identifies risks and inconsistencies and puts in place clearer structures or guidance.
5. Expert
Sets standards for how information is managed, organised and secured across teams. Ensures practices are consistent, secure and easy for everyone to follow.

Choosing the right digital tools

Selects the right tool for the task - from everyday choices about how to share or collaborate on a piece of work, through to contributing to team standards and organisational procurement decisions.

Skills

How would you rate your competence in deciding which tool or method best fits the task at hand?
1. Novice
Follows instructions about which tool to use for a task but needs support to understand why one option might suit better than another.
2. Awareness
Recognises that different tools suit different purposes and asks for guidance when unsure which to use. Begins to make simple choices independently, such as whether to email a document or share it in a collaborative space.
3. Working
Makes routine tool choices independently and with confidence - selecting the right platform, format or method for the task. Explains choices simply to colleagues and adapts when a chosen tool isn't working well.
4. Practitioner
Helps teams develop consistent, well-reasoned approaches to tool selection. Contributes to more formal requirements-gathering and advises colleagues on choices that balance user needs, security and organisational standards.
5. Expert
Sets team standards for tool selection and promotes evidence-based, consistent practice. Contributes to procurement and organisational buying decisions, ensuring choices reflect user needs, value for money and long-term fit.

Creating accessible digital content

Creates and shares digital content and communications that everyone can use, applying accessibility standards and meeting legal requirements to support clear, user-friendly digital experiences for all.

Knowledge

How would you rate your understanding of accessibility standards and legal requirements for digital content, and what they mean in practice?

Broader skills: Ethical Practice

1. Novice
Has little knowledge of accessibility standards or legal requirements but begins to notice when content might be hard for some people to use and shows curiosity about how to improve it.
2. Awareness
Understands what accessibility standards are, why they exist and what the key legal requirements mean for everyday content creation.
3. Working
Understands core accessibility principles and methods, including how standards apply across different content types, formats and platforms.
4. Practitioner
Has detailed knowledge of accessibility standards and legal requirements, including how they apply across varied tools, platforms and user needs.
5. Expert
Holds authoritative knowledge of accessibility standards, legal frameworks and emerging good practice, and understands their strategic implications for organisations.

Skills

How would you rate your competence in creating and checking digital content so it meets accessibility standards and works for people with different needs?

Broader skills: Ethical Practice

1. Novice
Follows clear instructions to create or edit simple digital content. Begins to notice when something might be hard to read or use and asks questions about how to make it easier.
2. Awareness
Uses basic accessibility steps with guidance such as adding alt text, using clear headings or checking colour contrast. Seeks help when unsure how to meet accessibility requirements.
3. Working
Applies accessibility principles in routine work, creating and editing content that meets departmental standards. Uses clear language, structured layouts and accessible formats. Makes simple improvements when issues are found.
4. Practitioner
Plans and delivers digital content that meets accessibility standards across different tools and platforms. Considers varied user needs, devices and assistive technologies when designing or reviewing content.
5. Expert
Leads accessible digital practice within individual teams. Sets and maintains best-practice efforts including training, ensures all content meets legal requirements, and reviews major projects for compliance and quality.

Behaviours

How would you rate your competence in supporting good accessibility practice beyond your own work - whether by modelling it, helping colleagues or flagging where standards aren't being met?

Broader skills: Ethical Practice, Collaborative Working

1. Novice
Begins to notice when colleagues produce content that might not work for everyone and asks questions about how to raise it.
2. Awareness
Raises observations about accessibility in team discussions and shares simple tips or guidance with colleagues when opportunities arise.
3. Working
Actively supports colleagues to meet accessibility standards. Shares examples of good practice, flags issues constructively and helps others understand why accessibility matters.
4. Practitioner
Leads on embedding accessible content practice across projects. Coaches colleagues, reviews others' work for accessibility and makes the case for prioritising it when it is deprioritised under delivery pressure.
5. Expert
Promotes a culture where accessibility is a default expectation. Ensures it is built into training, ways of working and major decisions, and holds teams accountable for meeting legal standards.

Responsible Information Practices

Handling information accurately and responsibly

Ensures the information you work with is accurate, current and well-managed - knowing where it came from, keeping it appropriately organised and protected, and disposing of it correctly when it is no longer needed.

Skills

How would you rate your competence in checking that the information you work with and share is accurate, current and from a reliable source?
1. Novice
Follows instructions to check simple information such as confirming where it came from or when it was last updated. Begins to notice when something looks wrong or out of date and asks for help to correct it.
2. Awareness
Uses basic checks to confirm that information is accurate and current, such as checking the source, looking for a version number or asking whether guidance has been updated. Recognises when information looks unreliable and reports it to others.
3. Working
Applies routine checks to keep information accurate and trustworthy. Verifies sources, identifies the authoritative version, corrects errors within their area of work and flags issues when information looks inconsistent or out of date.
4. Practitioner
Reviews how information is sourced, verified and maintained across projects. Identifies patterns of inaccuracy or unreliability, makes improvements and coaches colleagues to apply consistent checks. Ensures information used in decisions is traceable and well evidenced.
5. Expert
Sets standards for how information accuracy and reliability are maintained across teams. Ensures version control, provenance and currency are built into ways of working. Reviews major work to confirm information is well governed and promotes a culture of critical, evidence-based information use.
How would you rate your competence in organising, classifying and managing information and files so they are easy to find, appropriately protected and correctly disposed of when no longer needed?

Broader skills: Collaborative Working

1. Novice
Follows instructions to save files in the right place and begins to notice why organising information carefully matters. Needs support to understand classification or access settings.
2. Awareness
Saves documents in agreed locations, follows basic naming conventions and applies simple access or security settings with guidance. Understands that different information needs different levels of protection and asks for help when unsure.
3. Working
Organises and maintains files and shared spaces so information is easy to find and consistently structured. Applies correct classification and access controls, follows agreed retention and disposal processes and helps colleagues do the same.
4. Practitioner
Reviews and improves how information is organised, classified and managed across projects. Identifies risks such as incorrect classification, oversharing or information being retained longer than necessary. Coaches colleagues and ensures practices meet organisational standards.
5. Expert
Sets standards for how information is organised, classified, protected and disposed of across teams. Ensures classification, access control and retention policies are practical, consistently applied and built into team ways of working. Reviews major work to confirm information is well governed throughout its lifecycle.

Digital Transformation

Focusing on users

Understands the needs of the people your work affects and uses that understanding to improve how services and processes are designed and delivered.

Knowledge

How would you rate your understanding of user-centred principles and why understanding user needs matters for the work you do?
1. Novice
Has little knowledge of user-centred principles but begins to notice when something is hard to use and shows curiosity about why that matters.
2. Awareness
Understands the basic idea that services should be designed around the needs of the people who use them, and can explain why this leads to better outcomes.
3. Working
Understands core user-centred principles and methods, including how research and testing feed into design and delivery decisions.
4. Practitioner
Has detailed understanding of user-centred practice, including how to apply it across complex or multi-team projects and how to make the case for it to others.
5. Expert
Holds authoritative knowledge of user-centred principles and their strategic value, including how they connect to policy, delivery and public trust.

Skills

How would you rate your competence in finding out what users need - and using that insight to improve your work or processes?

Broader skills: Collaborative Working, Continuous Learning

1. Novice
Follows instructions to help gather simple feedback or observations but needs support to understand what to do with them.
2. Awareness
Helps collect feedback and takes part in simple research or testing activities. Shares observations with colleagues and recognises patterns in what users find difficult.
3. Working
Applies straightforward methods to gather and interpret user feedback. Shares insights with design or delivery colleagues and contributes to decisions about how to improve services.
4. Practitioner
Plans and runs research and testing activities, ensuring findings inform design and delivery. Coaches colleagues to apply user-centred methods consistently across projects.
5. Expert
Ensures research, testing and feedback are embedded in design and delivery processes across teams. Works with leaders to sustain a culture of continuous improvement in user experience.

Behaviours

How would you rate your ability to keep user needs in view when other priorities or pressures are pulling in a different direction?

Broader skills: Ethical Practice, Systems Thinking

1. Novice
Begins to notice when something doesn't seem to work well for users and asks questions about why decisions were made that way.
2. Awareness
Raises observations about user needs in team discussions and supports colleagues in considering the impact of decisions on users.
3. Working
Speaks up when user needs risk being overlooked in delivery decisions. Uses evidence from research or feedback to support their point of view.
4. Practitioner
Actively challenges decisions that deprioritise user needs and helps teams find ways to balance delivery pressures with user outcomes.
5. Expert
Models and promotes a culture where user needs are treated as non-negotiable. Supports leaders to make decisions that maintain public trust and service quality under pressure.

Making digital services work for everyone

Ensures digital public services work for everyone who needs them, reflecting the diverse needs, backgrounds and circumstances of all users and applying government guidance on inclusive design.

Knowledge

How would you rate your understanding of what inclusive design means for digital work and services, and what government guidance says about designing for everyone?

Broader skills: Ethical Practice

1. Novice
Has little knowledge of inclusive design principles but begins to notice when something might not work for certain users and asks questions about how to make it fairer.
2. Awareness
Understands what inclusive design means and why it matters - that services should work for everyone, including people who face barriers due to circumstances, disability, language or confidence.
3. Working
Understands core inclusive design principles and how government guidance applies them in practice, including what makes services hard to access for some people.
4. Practitioner
Has detailed knowledge of inclusive design requirements, including legal duties, good practice guidance and how to apply them across complex or multi-team work.
5. Expert
Holds authoritative knowledge of inclusive design principles, legal frameworks and emerging practice; understands their strategic implications for public service delivery.

Skills

How would you rate your competence in finding out about the different needs, backgrounds and circumstances of the people your work affects?

Broader skills: Ethical Practice, Systems Thinking

1. Novice
Follows instructions to help create or test content for different users but needs support to understand what makes something inclusive or exclusive.
2. Awareness
Uses basic inclusive design steps with guidance, such as checking that language, images or layouts work for different users. Identifies simple ways to make things clearer or more welcoming.
3. Working
Applies inclusive design principles in routine work; engages with different user groups, adapts approaches based on feedback and uses government guidance to make services easier to use for people with varied backgrounds and needs.
4. Practitioner
Plans and runs user research and engagement that includes people with varied backgrounds and experiences. Reviews services to check they reflect diverse user needs and leads on adopting inclusive design principles across work.
5. Expert
Sets the direction for inclusive digital design across teams. Ensures inclusivity is built into training and ways of working, and reviews major work to confirm it reflects the needs of all users - including those hardest to reach.
How would you rate your competence in using what you learn about users to shape or change your work?

Broader skills: Ethical Practice, Continuous Learning

1. Novice
Does not yet adapt work based on user feedback independently. Follows instructions to make suggested changes.
2. Awareness
Makes small changes to content or processes based on feedback or observations about what users find difficult.
3. Working
Applies what is learned from users to improve work. Tests changes, checks whether they help and iterates based on further feedback.
4. Practitioner
Leads improvement work based on user insight across a team; ensures changes are tested, evaluated and embedded.
5. Expert
Shapes how user insight drives continuous improvement across teams; ensures learning from diverse users is built into how services evolve.

Behaviours

How would you rate your ability to ensure the needs of people who are harder to reach or more likely to be excluded are considered in your work from the start?

Broader skills: Ethical Practice, Systems Thinking, Digital Wellbeing

1. Novice
Begins to notice when something might not work for certain users and asks questions about how to make it fairer or easier to use.
2. Awareness
Raises concerns when something seems likely to exclude certain users and looks for ways to make it more welcoming.
3. Working
Consistently applies an inclusive mindset in everyday work - questioning assumptions, sharing examples of good practice and encouraging others to consider who might be left out.
4. Practitioner
Leads by example on inclusive practice. Coaches colleagues to consider diverse user needs from the start and challenges approaches that risk excluding people. Reviews work to identify and address gaps.
5. Expert
Promotes a culture where inclusive thinking is a default expectation. Sets the standard for how teams consider excluded and harder-to-reach users and ensures this is built into training, ways of working and major decisions.

Digital Leadership

Leading in a digital age

Shapes how your team uses technology to work better and deliver more - championing digital, data and AI approaches, managing change confidently and building the capability and confidence of others.

Knowledge

How would you rate your understanding of how digital, data and AI can transform the way teams work and deliver outcomes?
1. Novice
Has little knowledge of how digital, data and AI can change the way teams work, but notices when technology makes tasks easier or faster and shows curiosity about how transformation happens.
2. Awareness
Understands that digital, data and AI can make work more efficient, fair and effective, and can describe in simple terms how technology has improved services or ways of working.
3. Working
Understands how digital, data and AI approaches can be applied to improve everyday work and service delivery, including common opportunities and barriers to adoption.
4. Practitioner
Has detailed knowledge of how to plan and deliver technology-enabled change, including how to balance innovation, risk, efficiency and public value.
5. Expert
Holds authoritative understanding of how digital, data and AI transformation works at a strategic level, including how to build long-term capability and align technology adoption with organisational priorities.

Skills

How would you rate your competence in encouraging and supporting colleagues to adopt digital, data and AI approaches in your work?

Broader skills: Collaborative Working, Continuous Learning

1. Novice
Follows instructions to use digital tools and systems that support change, and begins to notice opportunities where technology could improve outcomes.
2. Awareness
Uses digital tools in own work and shares ideas for small improvements. Recognises how technology can make work more efficient and seeks guidance on how to contribute to adoption.
3. Working
Applies digital, data and AI approaches to improve everyday work and contributes ideas for innovation. Helps colleagues try new tools responsibly and shares examples of how technology improves outcomes.
4. Practitioner
Plans and delivers technology-enabled change that balances innovation, efficiency and public value. Leads adoption initiatives, coaches colleagues and builds team confidence in new ways of working.
5. Expert
Sets the vision for digital, data and AI adoption across teams. Ensures technology-enabled change drives efficiency and aligns with organisational priorities, building long-term capability and confidence.

Behaviours

How would you rate your competence in helping colleagues feel confident about digital change - including when they are uncertain or sceptical?

Broader skills: Digital Wellbeing, Systems Thinking

1. Novice
Begins to notice when colleagues feel uncertain about new technology and asks questions about how to help them feel more confident.
2. Awareness
Recognises that people respond differently to digital change and supports colleagues by sharing simple, positive examples of technology improving work.
3. Working
Addresses concerns about new technology openly and honestly. Shares evidence of benefits and helps colleagues build confidence through practical experience and peer support.
4. Practitioner
Actively manages resistance to digital change by understanding its causes and adapting approaches to build trust. Creates opportunities for colleagues to develop confidence in new ways of working.
5. Expert
Models and promotes a culture where digital change is approached with curiosity and confidence. Ensures teams feel supported through transformation and that trust is built and maintained at every stage.
