AI

Using and governing artificial intelligence responsibly - including machine learning, automation, classification, recommendation and generative AI. This domain is here as AI appears in everyday tools and in higher-stakes services. Most colleagues need foundational literacy and sound judgement about outputs. Governance and ethics matter more when you use, commission or influence AI systems.

Questions are grouped as knowledge, skills or behaviours and rated against five level descriptors. Both are described on the proficiency levels page. Some questions are also tagged with broader skills.

Clusters

  2. AI Governance and Ethics

Foundational AI Skills

AI literacy

Builds foundational understanding of what AI is, how it works and what it can and cannot do across machine learning, automation and generative AI, and develops the practical skills needed to specify inputs, use tools effectively and work critically with outputs in everyday work.

Knowledge

How would you rate your understanding of what AI is, how it works and what it can and cannot do?
1. Novice
Basic or minimal knowledge; knows that AI tools exist and are used in government but has little understanding of how they work or what they can do.
2. Awareness
Understands what common AI tools do and broadly how they work; knows that AI outputs depend on inputs such as training data and that different AI approaches (e.g. prediction, classification, generative text) suit different tasks.
3. Working
Confidently understands how different AI types generate outputs, what their limitations are, and what failure modes mean for how outputs should be used - including hallucination and bias for generative systems, and false positives/negatives, drift and overfitting for prediction and classification systems.
4. Practitioner
Deep understanding of AI capabilities and limitations across different types including generative AI, predictive models, classification, recommendation and automation; advises colleagues on appropriate use and potential harms.
5. Expert
Authoritative knowledge of the AI landscape; shapes how AI is understood and used responsibly across teams and stays current with developments in AI capabilities and risks.

Skills

How would you rate your competence in using AI tools in your everyday work?
1. Novice
Uses AI tools with direct support; needs guidance on which tools are appropriate and how to use them for basic tasks.
2. Awareness
Uses familiar AI tools independently for simple, everyday tasks; knows which approved tools are available and follows organisational guidance on their use.
3. Working
Uses a range of AI tools confidently and independently in own work; selects the right tool for the task and applies AI where it genuinely adds value.
4. Practitioner
Uses AI tools fluently across varied contexts; helps colleagues identify and adopt tools that improve their work and shares practical knowledge of effective use.
5. Expert
Sets the standard for effective AI tool use across teams; ensures colleagues have the skills and confidence to use AI tools well and stays current with new tools and capabilities.
How would you rate your competence in specifying and preparing inputs so AI systems produce reliable, useful outputs?

Broader skills: Continuous Learning

1. Novice
Follows basic guidance on inputs - such as example prompts or provided reference data - with support; needs help understanding why inputs affect output quality.
2. Awareness
Prepares simple inputs independently - for example clear prompts with purpose and audience, or appropriate reference data - and understands that input quality shapes results.
3. Working
Specifies effective inputs for a range of everyday AI uses; for generative tools, frames prompts neutrally and iterates when outputs miss the mark; for other systems, recognises when training data, thresholds or context need checking; confirms outputs address the task before use.
4. Practitioner
Develops and shares effective input practices across AI types and contexts; coaches colleagues on clear specification and neutral framing where bias could be introduced; troubleshoots poor outputs by revisiting inputs and configuration.
5. Expert
Sets standards for how teams specify and validate inputs across AI systems; stays current with evolving practice; ensures colleagues understand how leading inputs or poor reference data can compromise high-stakes work such as policy advice.

Behaviours

How would you rate your competence in checking whether an AI output is reliable before acting on it or sharing it with others?

Broader skills: Ethical Practice

1. Novice
Beginning to notice that AI outputs might not always be right; tends to accept what AI produces without applying own judgment.
2. Awareness
Recognises that AI outputs can be wrong or misleading; approaches them with some caution and seeks guidance before acting on outputs that seem uncertain.
3. Working
Consistently approaches AI outputs critically; questions what AI produces, considers limitations and does not use outputs without applying own judgment.
4. Practitioner
Models critical engagement with AI outputs; challenges colleagues who accept AI outputs uncritically and promotes a questioning approach across the team.
5. Expert
Champions a culture of critical AI literacy across teams; ensures that questioning AI outputs is treated as standard practice rather than optional, especially in high-stakes decisions.

Assessing AI opportunities

Identifies where AI and automation could add genuine value, and recognises the limitations and risks that could make AI unsuitable.

Knowledge

How would you rate your understanding of where AI and automation can and cannot add value - including what kinds of tasks AI tends to help with and what makes some tasks unsuitable?
1. Novice
Basic or minimal knowledge; knows that AI can improve some processes but has little understanding of where it adds value or why it might not be suitable.
2. Awareness
Understands the types of tasks and processes where AI is most likely to add value; knows that suitability depends on factors like data quality, process stability and the nature of the task.
3. Working
Confidently understands where AI and automation can and cannot add value across a range of task types; knows what makes a task well-suited to AI and what constraints - technical, ethical or practical - might make it unsuitable.
4. Practitioner
Deep understanding of the conditions under which AI delivers genuine value; advises on where AI investment is likely to be worthwhile and where it is not, drawing on evidence and experience across different contexts.
5. Expert
Authoritative knowledge of AI opportunity and constraint across the full range of AI types; shapes how AI potential is assessed across teams and ensures adoption is evidence-based and proportionate.

Skills

How would you rate your competence in identifying tasks, inefficiencies or pain points that AI could help address?

Broader skills: Continuous Learning, Systems Thinking

1. Novice
Begins to notice that some tasks might benefit from AI; tends toward an all-or-nothing view of what AI can do rather than seeing specific opportunities clearly.
2. Awareness
Identifies simple opportunities where AI could reduce repetitive work or improve efficiency; shares ideas with colleagues and is open to exploring what might be possible.
3. Working
Actively identifies tasks, inefficiencies and pain points in own work and service processes where AI could add genuine value; develops initial ideas and engages others to test assumptions.
4. Practitioner
Leads structured identification of AI opportunities across a team or area of work; uses evidence and insight to pinpoint where AI could have the highest impact and builds a credible case for investment.
5. Expert
Shapes how AI opportunities are identified across teams; builds the culture, networks and frameworks needed to surface transformative ideas and turn them into lasting improvements.
How would you rate your competence in weighing up whether AI is the right solution for a problem, taking into account its limitations and risks alongside its potential benefits?

Broader skills: Systems Thinking, Ethical Practice

1. Novice
Aware that AI has some limitations but may not yet understand what they are; tends to either dismiss AI entirely or accept it uncritically rather than identifying specific risks or constraints.
2. Awareness
Recognises common constraints that affect AI suitability such as poor data quality, unstable processes or regulatory requirements; follows guidance to weigh these against potential benefits.
3. Working
Confidently identifies limitations and risks that could make AI unsuitable for a given task - including data gaps, ethical concerns, high-stakes decisions and tasks requiring human judgment; uses this to make well-rounded recommendations.
4. Practitioner
Anticipates complex constraints including ethical implications, change impact and long-term risk; helps colleagues recognise limitations they may have missed and ensures proposals are realistic and responsible.
5. Expert
Ensures AI is only adopted where limitations have been thoroughly assessed and mitigated; shapes how risk and constraint evaluation is done across teams and aligns adoption with strategy, ethics and regulation.

Using AI safely

Uses AI tools and automation safely in everyday work - knowing which tools are approved, what data can be shared with them, spotting when a prompt or task carries risk, and escalating when something feels wrong.

Knowledge

How would you rate your understanding of your organisation's rules on which AI tools are approved for use and what data can safely be shared with them?
1. Novice
Has little or no practical knowledge of which AI tools are approved or what data can safely be shared with them.
2. Awareness
Understands that rules exist on approved tools and data sharing, and knows where to find them.
3. Working
Understands which tools are approved for their own work and what data can and cannot be shared with them.
4. Practitioner
Has detailed understanding of the reasoning behind approval and data-sharing rules; explains them to colleagues and flags situations the guidance does not clearly cover.
5. Expert
Holds authoritative knowledge of approval and data-handling rules across tools and contexts; shapes or advises on the policy itself.

Skills

How would you rate your competence in spotting when a specific AI prompt or task carries a safety risk - whether from the data it contains or from the nature of the task itself - and adjusting or stopping before proceeding?

Broader skills: Ethical Practice, Systems Thinking

1. Novice
Cannot yet reliably spot when a prompt or task carries a safety risk without direct support; with support, begins to avoid obviously sensitive data in prompts.
2. Awareness
Uses basic checks with support to recognise common data risks in prompts; relies on others to judge whether a task itself is appropriate.
3. Working
Applies safety checks independently in routine situations, spotting both data exposure and tasks unsuited to AI, and adjusts or stops accordingly.
4. Practitioner
Uses judgement independently across varied scenarios to spot subtler risks that are not obviously about data; helps colleagues in their area of work see them too.
5. Expert
Leads application of safety judgement across teams or services in novel or ambiguous situations; shapes guidance or standards others rely on.

Behaviours

How would you rate your ability to escalate concerns about AI use to the right person when something feels wrong?

Broader skills: Ethical Practice, Collaborative Working

1. Novice
Shows curiosity and asks questions when something feels wrong, but needs prompting to know what to do about it.
2. Awareness
Raises concerns with a manager or colleague when encouraged, with support when needed; may hesitate to act alone without support.
3. Working
Escalates concerns about AI use consistently in everyday work, promptly and without needing to be prompted.
4. Practitioner
Coaches and supports others informally by spotting subtle or complex risks and escalating with enough detail for action to be taken.
5. Expert
Shapes standards by exercising authoritative judgement on when AI use crosses a line, including risks existing guidance does not yet cover.

AI Governance and Ethics

Interpreting AI outputs

Develops the skills and behaviours needed to check, interpret and communicate AI outputs critically and honestly - maintaining human judgment and transparency rather than deferring uncritically to AI recommendations.

Knowledge

How would you rate your understanding of how AI generates outputs and why they may be unreliable or biased?
1. Novice
Basic or minimal knowledge; knows that AI outputs can be wrong but may not understand why.
2. Awareness
Understands that AI outputs can be inaccurate, uncertain or biased; knows that outputs are shaped by the data the system was trained on and that checking them is important for fair and transparent decisions.
3. Working
Confidently understands why AI outputs may be unreliable - including hallucination, bias from training data, overconfidence and lack of context; knows how to recognise these issues and what they mean for how outputs should be used.
4. Practitioner
Deep understanding of how different AI types generate outputs, their specific failure modes and how patterns of error or bias can be identified and documented; the person others turn to when AI outputs need scrutinising.
5. Expert
Authoritative knowledge of AI output reliability, evaluation methods and documentation standards; shapes how outputs are reviewed, challenged and explained across teams and in high-stakes decisions.

Skills

How would you rate your competence in checking and interpreting AI outputs before acting on them?

Broader skills: Systems Thinking

1. Novice
Checks AI outputs with direct support; needs guidance to understand what an output means and whether it can be trusted.
2. Awareness
Applies basic checks to AI outputs with light support; asks questions about how results were generated and avoids acting on them without verifying.
3. Working
Checks and interprets AI outputs independently; compares them with other evidence, looks for errors or bias and applies consistent checks before acting.
4. Practitioner
Tests AI outputs rigorously across varied scenarios; identifies patterns of error or bias, suggests improvements and reviews colleagues interpretations for accuracy.
5. Expert
Sets standards for how AI outputs are checked and documented across teams; guides high-stakes decisions and ensures review processes are robust and consistently applied.

Behaviours

How would you rate your competence in making clear to others when a decision or recommendation has been informed by AI - including what that means for how certain you are?

Broader skills: Ethical Practice, Collaborative Working

1. Novice
Beginning to notice that AI involvement in a decision or output is worth mentioning; does not yet do this consistently or know how to explain uncertainty.
2. Awareness
Acknowledges when AI has contributed to a decision or recommendation and flags basic limitations to colleagues in plain terms.
3. Working
Consistently transparent about AIs role in decisions and recommendations; clearly communicates uncertainty and limitations so others can make informed judgments.
4. Practitioner
Actively ensures AI contributions and their limitations are clearly communicated in all relevant contexts; challenges colleagues who present AI outputs without appropriate caveats.
5. Expert
Champions transparency about AI use and uncertainty across teams; sets standards for how AI contributions are disclosed and documented, including in high-stakes decisions.
How would you rate your competence in deciding when to trust an AI output and when to question or override it?

Broader skills: Ethical Practice

1. Novice
Beginning to notice that AI outputs might not always be right; tends to accept what AI produces without applying own judgment.
2. Awareness
Recognises the importance of human judgment alongside AI; avoids accepting recommendations blindly and asks questions before acting on outputs.
3. Working
Consistently exercises own judgment when working with AI outputs; treats AI as one input among several rather than the final word, and overrides it when evidence or experience suggests otherwise.
4. Practitioner
Actively guards against automation bias in own and others work; coaches colleagues to apply independent judgment and challenges decisions that defer uncritically to AI.
5. Expert
Leads a culture of human oversight across teams; ensures AI is used as a tool to support judgment rather than replace it, particularly in high-stakes decisions affecting citizens.

Applying AI governance

Understands and applies the legal, regulatory and compliance frameworks that govern AI use in public services - including identifying and responding to governance risks.

Knowledge

How would you rate your understanding of the legal and regulatory frameworks that govern AI use?

Broader skills: Ethical Practice

1. Novice
Basic or minimal knowledge; knows that AI use is governed by rules and policies but has little understanding of what they are.
2. Awareness
Understands the key legal and regulatory requirements that apply to AI use; knows where to find guidance and who to ask when unsure.
3. Working
Confidently understands how relevant laws, regulations and organisational policies apply to AI use in own context; knows how governance frameworks connect to accountability and compliance, and recognises that rules are a minimum standard rather than the whole picture.
4. Practitioner
Deep understanding of AI governance frameworks, regulatory requirements and accountability structures; recognises when compliance alone is not enough and knows when to look beyond the rules.
5. Expert
Authoritative knowledge of AI law, regulation and governance; identifies gaps where existing frameworks have not kept pace with technology, advocates for stronger governance and shapes how frameworks are understood and applied across teams.

Skills

How would you rate your competence in applying governance and compliance requirements when using or commissioning AI?

Broader skills: Ethical Practice

1. Novice
Follows agreed rules and policies when using AI tools with direct support; asks for help when unsure whether something is permitted.
2. Awareness
Applies basic governance requirements reliably with light support; checks that AI use follows organisational policy and knows who to contact when requirements are unclear.
3. Working
Applies relevant governance and compliance requirements confidently in everyday AI use; ensures AI tools and outputs meet organisational and regulatory standards before acting on them.
4. Practitioner
Leads compliance across a team or area of work; ensures AI use meets all relevant legal and regulatory requirements and coaches colleagues on their governance obligations.
5. Expert
Sets governance standards for AI use across teams; ensures compliance frameworks are practical, up to date and consistently applied.
How would you rate your competence in identifying and responding to governance and compliance risks in AI use?

Broader skills: Ethical Practice, Systems Thinking

1. Novice
Recognises that some AI use may carry risks; flags concerns with a manager or appropriate contact with direct support.
2. Awareness
Identifies obvious governance risks such as using unapproved tools or sharing data inappropriately; escalates promptly and follows guidance on how to respond.
3. Working
Identifies and responds to governance risks in own AI use; applies agreed processes to mitigate risks and reports issues before they escalate.
4. Practitioner
Reviews AI use across a team or area of work for governance risks; leads responses to compliance issues and strengthens processes to prevent recurrence.
5. Expert
Shapes how governance risks are identified and managed across teams; ensures risk management processes are robust, proportionate and consistently applied.

Using AI responsibly and ethically

Applies ethical principles to AI use in public services - including fairness, transparency to citizens, public benefit and consideration of wider societal and environmental impact - grounded in the Data and AI Ethics Framework and UK AI regulatory principles.

Knowledge

How would you rate your understanding of the ethical principles that should guide responsible AI use in public services?

Broader skills: Ethical Practice

1. Novice
Has little knowledge of the ethical principles that apply to AI in public services, but begins to notice situations where AI use might raise concerns about fairness, transparency or harm - including environmental cost.
2. Awareness
Understands the key ethical principles that apply to AI - including fairness, transparency, avoiding harm to people and minimising environmental impact - and can recognise when they are relevant in simple situations.
3. Working
Applies ethical principles confidently in everyday AI use. Understands how fairness, transparency, wider societal impact and environmental sustainability apply to the AI tools and approaches used in own work.
4. Practitioner
Applies detailed knowledge of ethical principles across a team or area of work; ensures AI use reflects fairness, transparency, accountability and environmental responsibility, and stays current with evolving guidance and legislation.
5. Expert
Authoritative knowledge of ethical principles for AI in public services; shapes how they are understood and applied across teams, advocates for stronger standards where needed, and stays current with evolving guidance and legislation - including emerging requirements on environmental sustainability.

Skills

How would you rate your competence in applying fairness and public benefit considerations when using or recommending AI?

Broader skills: Ethical Practice, Systems Thinking

1. Novice
Recognises that AI can produce unfair or biased outcomes but needs support to identify when this is happening or what to do about it.
2. Awareness
Considers whether AI use is fair and likely to be beneficial; flags concerns when something seems discriminatory, harmful or contrary to the public interest, with guidance on how to respond.
3. Working
Applies fairness and public benefit considerations to own AI use; identifies when AI may produce inequitable outcomes for different groups and raises this appropriately before decisions are made.
4. Practitioner
Leads fairness and public benefit assessments across a team or area of work; ensures AI use actively serves the public interest, addresses bias and considers impact on different groups including those who may be disadvantaged.
5. Expert
Shapes how fairness and public benefit are assessed and embedded across teams; ensures AI adoption is genuinely equitable, challenges use that fails to meet this standard, and advocates for those most at risk of harm.
How would you rate your competence in making AI use transparent and explainable to those affected by it?

Broader skills: Ethical Practice, Collaborative Working

1. Novice
Recognises that people affected by AI decisions have a right to understand how those decisions were made; needs support to explain AI use to others in practice.
2. Awareness
Understands why transparency with affected people matters; can explain AI use in simple, honest terms with light guidance and knows when to seek help for more complex situations.
3. Working
Ensures people affected by AI-informed decisions in own work understand how AI was used and what it contributed; recognises that transparency about process and outcomes can and should be provided even where the technical detail of an AI system cannot be disclosed.
4. Practitioner
Ensures transparency and explainability are built into how AI is used and communicated across a team; helps colleagues explain AI use clearly to those affected, ensures people know how to raise concerns or challenge decisions, and understands which types of transparency are appropriate in which circumstances - including when technical transparency is not possible.
5. Expert
Sets standards for transparency and explainability in AI use across teams; ensures those affected by AI decisions can understand, question and challenge them through genuinely accessible routes, including in situations where full technical transparency is not possible for security or operational reasons - ensuring process and outcome transparency fill that gap.

Behaviours

How would you rate your ability to consider the wider and longer-term effects of using AI - including on people who won't be in the room when decisions are made?

Broader skills: Ethical Practice, Systems Thinking

1. Novice
Beginning to notice that AI can affect people beyond those directly involved; does not yet consider this consistently or know how to act on it.
2. Awareness
Considers the wider impact of AI use on people and communities, including those who may not have a say in how AI is used; raises concerns when impacts seem harmful or disproportionate.
3. Working
Consistently considers the wider and longer-term impact of AI in own work - including on people who are affected but have no voice in the decision - and raises this proactively rather than waiting to be asked.
4. Practitioner
Actively ensures the impact of AI on people with no direct voice is considered across a team or area of work; builds this into how decisions are made, ensures environmental and societal consequences are part of the conversation, and challenges decisions that overlook these impacts.
5. Expert
Champions consideration of wider societal and environmental impact in AI use across teams; creates the conditions for those with no voice to be represented in decision-making, takes long-term consequences seriously and holds the organisation accountable for the broader effects of its AI use.
How would you rate your ability to factor in the environmental cost of AI when deciding whether and how to use it?

Broader skills: Systems Thinking, Ethical Practice, Continuous Learning

1. Novice
Not yet aware that AI tools have an environmental cost. Begins to notice when this is mentioned and asks questions about what it means in practice.
2. Awareness
Understands that AI tools use significant energy and that choosing them proportionately is part of responsible use. Begins to consider whether AI is the right tool for a task before using it.
3. Working
Consistently considers whether AI is the most proportionate tool for a task - for example, using a search engine rather than a generative AI tool for a simple factual query. Avoids unnecessary or repeated AI use when simpler alternatives work just as well.
4. Practitioner
Actively promotes proportionate AI use across a team or area of work. Reviews how AI tools are used, identifies patterns of unnecessary or wasteful use, and coaches colleagues to make more sustainable choices. Flags environmental considerations when AI adoption decisions are being made.
5. Expert
Embeds environmental sustainability into team expectations and standards for AI use. Ensures that guidance, training and procurement processes reflect the principle that AI should be used proportionately and that its resource costs are a legitimate factor in adoption decisions. Connects this to broader organisational commitments on sustainability.
